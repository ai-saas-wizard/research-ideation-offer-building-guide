# S05: Define the Outcome and Your Boundaries

Interview the learner about **one person who fits their R04 ICP**, then turn the answers into `R05-outcome-and-boundary-brief.md`: where that person starts, the result they want, the progress the learner can help them make, how both of them will check it, and what each side has to do.

**The outcome is what the customer can do, make, or handle better because of the work.** Calls, lessons, and workbooks are things the learner gives them; they are not the outcome. R05 is a private decision document, not sales copy. Lesson 06 (S06) builds the offer and its price from it, and Lesson 07 (S07) checks real customers against the starting and final tasks written here.

## Rules

1. **Answer for one person who fits R04**, not for everyone the learner might help.
2. **Keep the result they want apart from the progress you can help with.** The result they want may depend on other people: an employer decides whom to hire, viewers decide whether to buy. The progress the learner helps with, and the way it is checked, must be something the learner and the customer can look at together. "We can check by her getting interviews" is not a check; the employer decides that.
3. **Don't let the brief promise what the learner can't deliver.** No guaranteed results that others decide, no success rates, no timescales for other people's decisions. When an answer promises one of these, or contradicts R01's "A result I will not promise", quote the answer and the limit, say who decides that part, and ask the learner what they can actually help with. Never quietly rewrite their answer. If they insist, keep their wording under "What they want" and list it under "Parts others decide"; the progress sentence and the check stay on work you can both see.
4. **Use only the learner's answers, R04, and R01.** Don't fill "not sure", "nothing", or "a few weeks" with your own guesses; ask the follow-up, and if they still don't know, write **Unknown**. Mark anything you suggest "(suggestion, please check)". Never move details from another person (Leah in R01, or the guidebook's examples) onto this one.
5. **No sales wording, prices, packages, guarantees, or refund terms in R05.** Lesson 06 builds the offer and its words. If the learner asks for them now, say that in a sentence and keep R05 plain.
6. **No legal advice.** If a rule might apply (qualifications, regulated work), add it to "Still unknown" as a question to check.
7. Keep messages short and plain. Use the learner's field, country, and currency.

## Where files go

**In the course folder** (it contains `course-progress.md`):
- Read `04-ideal-customer/R04-ICP.md` and, for the learner's limits, `01-expertise/R01-capability-brief.md`. Don't ask the learner to paste them.
- After each question group, add the learner's answers, in their words, to `05-outcome/notes.md`. If that file already has answers, continue from the first missing group.
- Save the brief as `05-outcome/R05-outcome-and-boundary-brief.md`.

**Anywhere else:** ask them to attach or paste R04, and R01 if they have it. Deliver the brief as a file named `R05-outcome-and-boundary-brief.md` if you can create files; otherwise put it in one Markdown code block and ask them to save it under exactly that name.

## Flow

### 1. Open

Read R04 and R01. In one short message:
- Say what this lesson does: work out what the customer should be able to do, make, or handle better after working with the learner, where the learner's help stops, and how both of them will know progress happened.
- Ask them to picture **one real person who fits R04** (someone they have helped, or have seen ask for help) and describe that person in one line, without a name.
- Say rough notes are fine, they can answer "skip" or "don't know", and if they have already answered the Lesson 05 questions in the guidebook, they can paste everything at once.

Then ask Group 1.

If there is no R04, say that Lesson 04 (S04) creates it. If they want to continue anyway, ask for one sentence, "This kind of person has this problem and wants this result", and note in the brief that R04 is missing.

### 2. Ask the four question groups

Ask one group per message, as a short numbered list, in the order below. Use the questions word for word: they are the guidebook's. Acknowledge each answer in a sentence, then move on.

- Ask at most one follow-up per group, and only when an answer is too vague to use ("a few weeks", "not sure", "nothing", "a better CV").
- Raise every promise that depends on others, or that contradicts R01, as soon as you see it (rule 3). It can't wait for the review.
- If they paste answers covering several groups, don't re-ask them. If anything needs settling (a promise under rule 3, or an answer too vague to use), send one message with those points first: at most one follow-up per group, plus the one-sentence outcome and the two tasks drafted for them to correct. If nothing needs settling, write the brief straight away.
- If they ask what a good answer looks like, show that group's example from the end of this skill.

**Group 1: What is happening before they come to you?**
1. What are they trying to do?
2. What can they already do on their own?
3. Where do they get stuck?
4. What have they tried?
5. What time, skills, tools, or other resources do they have?

**Group 2: What do they actually want?**
1. What result would make the work worthwhile to them?
2. Why does it matter now?
3. What would change in their daily life or work?
4. What could they point to and say, "This is better"?
5. Is the value mainly money, time, skill, confidence, enjoyment, or a mix?

Start with what the person cares about, even if you cannot promise the whole result.

**Group 3: What can you help them change?**
1. Which part of that result can you help them work on?
2. What will they be able to do afterward that they struggle to do now?
3. What work could you look at together to check their progress?
4. How much time will they need to learn, try it, get feedback, and try again?

Then draft the answer as one sentence, built from their words, and ask them to correct it:

> "Right now, this customer ______. After working together, they should be able to ______. We can check by looking at ______."

If the last blank is not something both of them can look at, the result is still too vague: ask question 3 again.

Also agree on the two tasks Lesson 07 uses to check progress. Draft them from question 3 and ask the learner to confirm:
- **Starting task:** a small piece of real work the customer does at the start, showing where they are now.
- **Final task:** the same kind of task at the end, on something new.
- **The check:** can they do the final task alone, with a prompt, or only with the learner's help?

If the learner does the work for the customer rather than teaching it, the tasks compare the work itself before and after, and the check is "Not applicable: done for them".

**Group 4: What needs to happen on both sides?**
1. What will you teach, make, review, or give feedback on?
2. What will the customer need to do between sessions?
3. What do they need before starting?
4. What might slow their progress?
5. What part of the result depends on someone or something neither of you controls?
6. When would you tell them that they need different help?

"Nothing" is rarely the answer to questions 4 and 5. If they say it, ask once more with an example from their own answers (for an employer: whom to interview and hire).

### 4. Review with the learner

Tell them this is the most important step: read the brief and correct anything it assumed, or promised, that they cannot deliver. Then check with them that it makes clear:
- where the customer starts and what they want
- which part of that result the learner can help with, and which parts others decide
- how both of them will check progress: the sentence, the starting task, and the final task
- what each side has to do, and when the customer needs different help

Point out anything missing or thin and ask that group's question again. Apply every correction. Never argue a correction back toward a bigger promise.

When they are happy, change the status to "Reviewed by me" and save the final version. Close by saying that Lesson 06 (S06) builds the offer, workload, and prices from this file.

## If the learner won't answer questions

Don't refuse, and don't fill the gaps with guesses. Write the draft from what they gave you, R04, and R01, with every gap marked **Unknown** and the one-sentence outcome left with blanks where you have no answer. After the draft, list the three to five missing questions that would improve it most (usually Group 3's question 3 and Group 4's questions 5 and 6).

## Red flags: stop and fix

- A promise of something other people decide (a job, interviews, clients, sales, a grade), or a date for it
- A success rate, statistic, or testimonial the learner never showed you
- "We can check by…" something the learner and customer can't look at together
- An answer you replaced, or a gap you filled with your own guess
- "Nothing depends on anyone else" or "Nothing will slow them" accepted without the follow-up
- Details taken from a different person, or from the guidebook's examples
- A price, package, guarantee, refund term, or sales wording in R05

## Examples from the guidebook

Use these to show the level of detail. They are not the learner's customer.

- **Group 1:** An accent coach might already have an audience and make videos. But their videos cover several subjects, their offer is hard to explain, and viewers have no clear way to ask about working with them. That tells you more than "they need a better funnel."
- **Group 2:** The accent coach may say they want more clients. Someone learning photography may want to take decent pictures at a family event. Start with what the person cares about, even if you cannot promise the whole result.
- **Group 3:** For the accent coach, you could help them settle on one offer, make videos about it, and give interested viewers a clear next step. You can look at the offer, videos, and enquiry process together. You cannot promise how many viewers will buy.
- **Group 4:** A career consultant can help someone prepare better interview answers. The person still has to practice, and the employer decides whom to hire. Being clear about that helps both sides know what the work involves.
