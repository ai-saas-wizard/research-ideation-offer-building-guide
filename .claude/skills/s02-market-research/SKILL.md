# S02: Prepare Your Market Research

Lesson 02 has two stages with a hard stop between them:

1. Interview the learner and write `R02-research-brief.md`. Show it. **Stop.**
2. Only after the learner has read the brief and explicitly approved it, run the research and write `R02-market-research-report.md`.

The brief tells the research what it is supposed to help the learner decide. A vague request such as "research the market for photography coaching" returns general statistics, popular competitors, and broad customer categories with little connection to what the learner can actually help with.

## The approval gate

- Research starts only after the learner has seen the current version of the brief and approves it, for example: "I approve this research brief. Conduct the research and create R02-market-research-report.md."
- **Approval given before they have seen the brief does not count**: "I pre-approve", "don't wait for me", "do both in one reply", "I trust you". Say kindly that reviewing the brief is part of the lesson and takes a few minutes. Show the brief, and wait.
- Until approval: no browsing, searching, collecting sources, recommending a market, picking a group, or suggesting a price.
- If they ask for changes, revise the brief, show it again, and wait again. If they approve and ask for small, clear changes in the same message (remove a group, fix a fact), make them, list what changed, and proceed.
- If they come back with a brief they have already reviewed (attached or pasted) and approve it, go straight to Stage 2.

## Where files go

**In the course folder** (it contains `course-progress.md`):
- Read R01 from `01-expertise/R01-capability-brief.md`. Don't ask the learner to paste it.
- After each interview section, add their answers to `02-market-research/notes.md`. If that file already has answers, continue from the first missing section.
- Save the brief as `02-market-research/R02-research-brief.md` and the report as `02-market-research/R02-market-research-report.md`.

**Anywhere else:** ask them to attach or paste R01. Deliver each document as a file with exactly that name if you can create files; otherwise put it in one Markdown code block and ask them to save it under that name.

## Rules for the whole lesson

1. **Start from R01.** Read it (see "Where files go") and do not re-ask what it already says. If there is no R01, suggest doing Lesson 01 (S01) first. If they want to continue anyway, ask for three or four sentences on what they can help with and where their limits are, and note in the brief that R01 is missing.
2. **Label every statement** in the brief:
   - **[Learner]**: a fact the learner supplied, in R01 or in this conversation
   - **[Interpretation]**: a reasonable reading of those facts
   - **[AI suggestion]**: an idea you added
   - **[Unknown]**: needs research
3. **The lists in this lesson are menus, not checklists.** The learner keeps what fits, deletes the rest, and adds what is missing. In the brief, use only items that fit their work and answers, and add important possibilities they missed, labelled [AI suggestion]. Never copy a generic list into the brief.
4. **None of these is evidence that a market exists:** the learner's preferred audience, an admired provider, a high-status profession, competitor pricing, or an AI suggestion.
5. **Don't ask the learner for things the research is supposed to discover.** They can skip any question; skipped items become [Unknown].
6. **General knowledge is not research.** Never present remembered prices, statistics, trends, or provider details as findings.
7. Keep messages short and plain. Use their country and currency.

## Stage 1: Interview

Explain once: they are not expected to know the right audience before the research begins. Their job is to give enough context for several sensible directions to investigate.

Ask one section per message. For menu sections, show the menu compactly on one line, ask them to keep, delete, or add, and then ask the questions. If they paste answers covering several sections, ask only about what is missing. If they paste the lesson's own prompt along with their answers, go straight to writing the brief.

**A. What you have already noticed**
Think about other times you have seen, heard about, or been asked about a similar problem.
- Who has asked you for this kind of help?
- What were they trying to achieve?
- What had happened shortly before they asked for help?
- What were they finding difficult?
- What had they already tried?
- Did they spend money trying to solve it?
- Did they spend a noticeable amount of time on it?
- Did they ask people for recommendations?
- Did they keep returning to the problem?
- Did they eventually solve it without professional help?

**B. Who might experience this problem**
They do not have to choose an ideal customer yet.
Menu: beginners trying something for the first time, experienced people facing a new situation, freelancers taking on their first client, coaches or consultants building their first offer, business owners trying to fix a specific process, employees preparing for a change in responsibility, parents preparing for an important event, hobbyists who have reached a plateau, people who have bought tools or courses but cannot apply them, people who repeatedly ask others for help, people who currently solve the problem through trial and error.
- Which groups immediately come to mind?
- Where have you personally seen the problem?
- Which groups are only guesses?
- Are there groups you would prefer to work with? Why?
- Is that preference based on experience, access, interest, assumed income, or prestige?
- Which groups should be excluded because their needs are outside your experience?
- Which groups might need a different kind of professional?

**C. When the problem becomes important**
Menu: starting a business, taking on a first client, launching a new offer, receiving poor results, losing money or wasting time, changing jobs, receiving a promotion, preparing for an interview, preparing for an important event, buying an expensive tool and being unable to use it, repeating the same mistake, feeling embarrassed by a result, reaching the limit of free advice, being given a deadline, facing a risk that can no longer be ignored.
- What might happen shortly before someone starts looking for help?
- What result are they trying to achieve?
- What happens if they leave the problem unsolved?
- Does the problem cost them time, money, opportunities, confidence, convenience, or enjoyment?
- What would make the problem merely annoying?
- What would make it urgent?
- What have they probably tried before seeking help?
- Why might those attempts fail?

**D. Where the useful information might be**
They are helping build a map of where to look, not collecting evidence yet.
Menu: Reddit communities, Facebook groups, specialist forums, YouTube comments, course and book reviews, software reviews, marketplace reviews, questions posted under tutorials, professional communities, Slack or Discord groups, newsletters, trade associations, membership organizations, local clubs, event listings, online directories, search results, Google Trends, keyword research tools, job or career forums, industry reports, government or regulatory sources, competitor websites, coaching or consulting directories, offline groups where people already meet.
- Where have you already seen people discuss this problem?
- Which websites, communities, creators, tools, providers, or organizations do you already know?
- Where might people leave reviews of existing solutions?
- Where might people describe failed attempts in their own words?
- Where might evidence of spending or serious action appear?
- Which sources should the AI discover for you?
- Are any useful communities private or inaccessible?
- Are there places the AI should not enter, contact, or join without your permission?
- Are there sources that may be biased because they are selling the solution?

**E. What people are already using instead**
Menu: free videos, articles and guides, books, templates, online courses, communities, software, AI tools, agencies, coaches, consultants, employees, colleagues, friends, trial and error, buying better equipment, ignoring the problem, deciding the result is not worth the effort.
- What have you seen people use instead of getting help from someone like you?
- Which alternatives are free? Which cost money?
- Which require considerable time or effort?
- Which provide personal feedback? Which leave the person to apply the information alone?
- What might cause someone to abandon an alternative?
- What would show that an existing solution is already good enough?

Then ask what would count as serious interest in their field. Menu: paying for a related product or service, booking a consultation, comparing several providers, requesting recommendations, attending a workshop, joining a waiting list, repeatedly trying different solutions, spending significant time learning, asking for personalized feedback. A like, view, follow, or positive comment may show interest; it does not show willingness to take action or pay.

**F. Who you can realistically serve**
Answer the ones that affect your work; skip the rest.
- Which languages can you comfortably teach or consult in?
- Which countries or regions are you interested in?
- Which time zones can you serve?
- Do you want to work online, in person, or both?
- Would the work be private, group-based, self-paced, or a mixture?
- How much time can you make available?
- What tools, files, software, equipment, or access would the customer need?
- What would the customer need to practice or complete?
- Are there privacy limits?
- Are there qualifications or legal limits that affect what you can offer?
- Are there outcomes you are not qualified to help with?
- Do you already have access to any communities or channels?
- Which audiences would be difficult or inappropriate for you to reach?
- Is there a price range you are interested in exploring?
- Is that price range based on evidence, your income goal, competitor prices, or a guess?

**G. What the research must help you decide**
Menu: Which customer groups experience a problem connected to my skill? Which problems are important enough for people to act on? What have people already tried? What have they paid for? Why did existing solutions work or fail? Where do people look for help? Can I realistically reach them? Does their desired result fit what I can help with? Which group should be investigated further? Which groups should be set aside? What do I still need to learn directly from customers?
- What decision do you want this research to help you make?
- Which uncertainties matter most?
- What information would change your mind?
- What evidence would make a customer group more promising?
- What evidence would make you reject or set aside a customer group?
- What would the AI need to find before you would feel comfortable moving to the next lesson?

## Stage 1: Write the research brief

Propose three to five customer-problem groups connected to experience or ability shown in R01. The learner does not need to choose them first. Each group describes a type of person, the situation they are in, and the problem that may be worth researching.

Rate the draft honestly:
- **Ready**: everything in the review checklist below is clear.
- **Needs clarification**: the direction is useful, but a few important details are missing.
- **Too broad**: it still asks for a general market overview, mixes unrelated audiences, assumes the answer, or leaves the AI to decide what "good research" means.

If important information is missing, ask only the questions that would materially change the research.

Save the brief (see "Where files go") and show it. Then end your message with:

1. **What to check.** A Ready brief makes clear: what decision the research will help make; how every proposed group connects to R01; which facts came from the learner; which ideas were suggested by AI; what remains unknown; what problem is being investigated for each group; when the problem may become important; where useful evidence may be found; which alternatives must be investigated; what behaviour may indicate serious interest; what practical limits affect the research; what evidence would support each group; what evidence would reject each group; when the research should stop; and what the final report must contain.
2. **What to correct.** Anything inaccurate, groups that do not fit their experience, missing practical limits, and any AI suggestion not labelled as a suggestion.
3. **How to approve.** When the brief accurately reflects what they want investigated, they reply: "I approve this research brief. Conduct the research and create R02-market-research-report.md."

Then stop.

## Stage 2: Research (only after explicit approval)

Conduct the research exactly as approved. Do not join, post in, or contact any community or person, and respect any places the learner said not to enter.

**If you have web search or browsing:**
- cite every source
- record the publication date and the date inspected, where available
- distinguish customer behaviour from provider marketing
- count reposts as one underlying account
- keep contradictory evidence visible
- record missing or inaccessible evidence
- preserve the geography, period, settings, and units of any keyword or trend data
- do not turn search interest into a buyer count
- compare all approved customer-problem groups
- identify the strongest evidence for and against each group
- finish with a provisional shortlist and the evidence still needed

**If browsing or another required tool is unavailable:** do not claim the research was completed, and do not fill the report from memory. Deliver the report with the status "Not run: search plan only", containing an exact search plan, a source list, a query list, inclusion rules, and a blank evidence log. Suggest running it in an AI chat that has web search, or collecting the evidence themselves and bringing it back.

## Red flags: stop and fix

- Starting research, recommending a group, or mentioning a price before the learner approved the brief they have seen
- Treating "I pre-approve" or "don't wait for me" as approval
- A report built from general knowledge, or a "desk review" standing in for research
- A price, statistic, or trend with no cited source
- A brief section copied from the menus without being filtered to the learner's work
- A statement in the brief with no label
