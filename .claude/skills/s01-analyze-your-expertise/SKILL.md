# S01: Analyze Your Own Expertise

Interview the learner about **one real time they helped someone**, then turn their answers into `R01-capability-brief.md`. Every later lesson (S02 onward) builds on this file, so it has to be accurate before it can be useful.

**R01 is a private decision document, not marketing.** An inflated R01 sends the learner toward the wrong customers, the wrong offer, and claims they cannot back up. Sales wording comes in later lessons.

## What the lesson is looking for

Labels are too broad to build an offer on: "I'm a photographer", "I'm good at marketing", "I've worked in this industry for ten years". A useful capability is a real problem the learner has learned to handle, for example: *"I can look at an indoor photograph, work out why it is blurred, explain the cause, and show someone how to correct it."*

Keep four things apart. They overlap, but they are not the same:
- **Experience:** what they have been exposed to and learned through doing.
- **Skill:** what they can do consistently enough that another person could rely on their help.
- **Teachability:** whether they can help someone else understand the problem, make decisions, practice, and improve.
- **Demand:** whether other people care enough about the problem to act on it.

Perspective shift: experience runs from A (beginner) to Z (maestro). The learner might be at H. They do not need to be at Z to help someone at B reach D. The useful question is: *"What meaningful steps do I understand well enough to help someone else take them?"*

## Rules

1. **Use only what the learner supplied**: their answers, and their CV if they shared one. Never add duties, methods, numbers, dates, credentials, feedback, or results, even ones that are typical for their job.
2. **Keep every claim at the strength they gave it.** "She got three interviews after we changed her CV" stays exactly that. It does not become "I turn around stalled job searches" or "a proven, repeatable method".
3. **Mark every gap.** Anything missing or uncertain is written as **Unknown**. If they have never seen someone apply their help without them taking over, write **Teaching ability untested**. If they cannot remember the order of their attempts, say so.
4. **Lead with the problem they handled, not with years or job titles.** Years in a field are context, not the capability.
5. **If asked to make it "impressive", "professional", or "expert-sounding"**, explain in one or two sentences why R01 has to stay accurate, then keep it accurate. Do not pad it with impressive numbers (years, placements, clients) that do not show this specific capability.
6. **Keep messages short and plain.** The learner is not technical. Use their field, country, and currency. The course's Maya examples only show the level of detail expected; never copy them into the learner's brief.

## Where files go

**In the course folder** (it contains `course-progress.md`):
- Before you open, read any CV in `00-about-me/`. If there is none, mention they can drop one there.
- After each question group, add the learner's answers, in their words, to `01-expertise/notes.md`. If that file already has answers, continue from the first missing group.
- Save the brief as `01-expertise/R01-capability-brief.md`.

**Anywhere else:** they can paste or upload their CV if they want to. Deliver the brief as a file named `R01-capability-brief.md` if you can create files; otherwise put it in one Markdown code block and ask them to save it under exactly that name.

## Conversation flow

### 1. Open

In one short message:
- In a sentence or two, say what you are doing together: finding the part of their experience that is specific enough to teach, useful enough to matter, and real enough to test.
- Offer an optional step: they can share their CV for context (see "Where files go").
- Ask them to pick **one time they actually helped someone** with the thing they want to consult on. It could be a client, friend, colleague, family member, or themselves, formally or informally. Not their whole career.
- Tell them rough notes and rambling are fine, and they can answer "skip" or "don't know" to anything.
- If they have already answered the Lesson 01 questions in the workbook, they can paste everything at once.

Then ask Group 1.

### 2. Ask the eight question groups

Ask one group per message, as a short numbered list, in the order below. Acknowledge each answer in a sentence, then move on.

- Ask at most one follow-up per group, and only when an answer is too vague to use ("I helped with photos" → "What exactly was going wrong in the photos?").
- If they drift into their career history, steer them back to the one situation.
- If they paste answers that cover several groups, do not re-ask those. Ask only for the missing groups.
- A CV can help them choose which situation to talk about. It never answers these questions for them.
- If they ask what a good answer looks like, or give a one-line answer, show that group's example from the end of this skill.

**Group 1: Choose one person and the job**
1. Who were you helping?
2. What were they trying to get done?
3. What were you responsible for?
4. What made the work difficult?
5. What limits or constraints were you dealing with?
6. What happened at the beginning?

**Group 2: Walk through what happened**
1. What did you try first?
2. What happened?
3. What did you try next?
4. Which attempts failed or only partly worked?
5. What did you think the problem was at the time?

Keep the attempts in order. If you cannot remember the exact order, say so.

**Group 3: Explain what changed your approach**
1. What did you eventually notice that you had missed before?
2. What changed your mind?
3. Which decision, check, or rule did you change?
4. What would you look at first if the same problem happened today?

Don't only describe the final result. Explain how you learned to understand what was really happening.

**Group 4: Describe what you can do now**
1. If someone gave you the same problem today, what would you look at first?
2. What would you do next, and why?
3. What can you now notice, decide, explain, or demonstrate that you could not do before?
4. Which part of this could a beginner realistically learn from you?

**Group 5: Show what backs this up**
1. What work samples, repeated results, feedback, demonstrations, or before-and-after examples support what you are saying?
2. What does each example show?
3. What does it not show?
4. Which parts are based mainly on memory or confidence rather than something another person could inspect?

Do not make up proof. If something has not been tested, say so.

**Group 6: See whether you can help someone else**
1. What could the person do before your help?
2. What did you explain, demonstrate, or ask them to practice?
3. What could they do afterward without you taking over?
4. Where did they still struggle?

If you have never observed this clearly, the answer is "teaching ability untested".

**Group 7: Be clear about your limits**
1. Who could you realistically help?
2. What would they need to know or be able to do before starting?
3. Which situations are outside your experience?
4. What result would you feel comfortable helping someone work toward?
5. What result would you not promise?

**Group 8: Look for signs that people care**
1. Who has asked you about this problem before?
2. What were they trying to achieve?
3. What had they already tried?
4. What made the problem important enough for them to seek help?
5. Which parts have you actually seen, and which parts are still assumptions?

### 4. Review with the learner

After the draft, tell them this is the most important step: read it and correct anything that does not accurately represent what happened. Then check with them that the brief makes clear:

- who they helped and what that person needed
- what they actually did
- what changed
- what they can do now
- what supports the claim
- what remains untested
- who they can help
- what they will not promise

Point out any item that is missing or thin, and ask that group's question again. Apply every correction. Never argue a correction back toward a stronger claim.

When they are happy, change the status line to "Reviewed by me" and save the final version (see "Where files go").

Close by saying that Lesson 02 (S02) starts from this file.

## If the learner won't answer questions

Don't refuse, and don't fill the gaps with guesses. Write the draft from what they gave you, with every gap marked **Unknown**. After the draft, list the three to five missing questions that would improve it most (usually from Groups 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8), and say they can answer them any time to complete the brief.

## Red flags: stop and fix the draft

- A sentence describing work, knowledge, or methods the learner never mentioned
- "Expert", "proven", "track record", "repeatable", "transform", or "guarantee", unless the learner said it and showed it
- Years of experience or a job title used as the headline capability
- A heading deleted instead of marked **Unknown**
- A cause-and-effect claim ("my changes got her the job") that the learner did not make
- A suggestion to add numbers or testimonials just to sound more convincing

## Level-of-detail examples (Maya, invented)

Maya is the course's invented photographer. Use these only to show how much detail is useful. They are not the learner's experience.

- **Group 1:** I was helping a friend take indoor photos of his family. The room looked bright, but the pictures were often blurry. He had a basic adjustable camera, limited lighting, and did not understand the settings yet.
- **Group 2:** At first I thought he was choosing the wrong focus point, so I changed the autofocus setting; still blurry. Then I increased sharpness afterward, which only made it look harsher. Comparing several photos, I saw that some had camera movement and others had movement from the person.
- **Group 3:** I realized "blurry" did not always mean the same thing. I started checking whether the whole image was blurred or only the moving person, and how much light there was. I stopped changing settings randomly and diagnosed the likely cause first.
- **Group 4:** I would first check which parts are blurred, whether the subject was moving, how much light was available, and which shutter speed was used. A beginner could learn to tell camera movement from subject movement.
- **Group 5:** I have before-and-after photos from several indoor sessions. They show I can fix the problem in my own work, and my friend took clearer photos after my explanation. They do not prove I can teach a complete paid course or help every beginner apply the method alone.
- **Group 6:** Before, my friend changed settings randomly and could not explain the blur. I showed him how to compare sharp and blurred parts, then had him shoot at different shutter speeds. Afterward he could spot some camera movement himself, but still needed help with moving subjects in low light.
- **Group 7:** I could help beginners who want clearer everyday indoor photos and have a camera with adjustable settings. I would not claim to prepare someone for professional event photography, studio lighting, or perfect results in every condition.
- **Group 8:** Two friends asked me why their indoor photos were blurry; both had already tried changing focus settings or editing afterward. I have seen the problem in those two people. I do not yet know how common it is or whether anyone would pay for help.
